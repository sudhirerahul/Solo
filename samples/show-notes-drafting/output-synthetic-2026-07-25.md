# Building the Boring Parts First

**Host:** Jordan Calloway **Guest:** Theo Okonkwo, founder of Runway Kit

## Summary

Theo Okonkwo didn't set out to start a company — he was doing freelance ops consulting for small trucking outfits when a scheduling problem he solved for one client turned into Runway Kit. He walks through the two decisions that shaped the business: ripping out a licensed routing engine to build his own from scratch, and a pricing rollout that cost him a customer he actually liked. It's a candid look at what going solo really costs, and what it teaches you when nobody else is there to catch the mistake before it lands.

## Show Notes

**Origin story.** Theo Okonkwo, founder of Runway Kit, started out doing freelance ops consulting for small trucking companies (five to fifteen trucks, no software budget). Dispatchers were coordinating with whiteboards and group texts because every product on the market was built for fleets of five hundred trucks, not five. He built a scheduling tool for one client to solve their problem; a competitor asked for it next, and by the time he had four customers and a spreadsheet invoicing system, he realized he had a company. He didn't write a pitch deck until eighteen months in — his first real "company" artifact was a shared folder of screenshots of customers' dispatch whiteboards.

**Solo by accident, then by choice.** Okonkwo built Runway Kit alone from the start, initially just because he didn't know anyone else who wanted to spend a year on trucking dispatch software. Around his seventh or eighth customer, people kept asking if he had a co-founder lined up, as if it were assumed he'd need one eventually; sitting with that question, he realized the honest answer was that he'd only ever been told he should want one, and stopped treating solo as a temporary state he was passing through.

**Building the engine in-house.** In early 2022, Runway Kit piloted a licensed routing engine from a company called Fleetward with two customers for about six weeks. It technically worked but optimized for highway miles, not for how small operators actually think — which driver is close to home tonight, which driver hasn't had a rest day in two weeks. Runway Kit ripped it out and built its own scheduling core from scratch starting April 2022, a five-month effort Okonkwo calls the most expensive decision of the company's first two years — and the reason customers stay. His advisor, Priya Chen, who had run ops at a much larger logistics company, thought the rebuild was too risky for a team of one. At month three, burned through his runway buffer and hitting edge cases (split shifts, Spanish-language requests, drivers working two operators at once), Okonkwo called Priya Chen doubting the decision; she asked only whether the Fleetward pilot customers were happier with the broken new prototype. They were, so he kept going.

**The pricing mistake.** In fall 2022, Okonkwo switched Runway Kit's pricing overnight from a flat monthly fee to per-truck pricing, announced by email with just eleven days' notice. Three customers churned immediately, including his second-ever customer, who called not to yell but to say she'd been notified like a vendor instead of talked to like a person. He'd optimized the timing for his own finance deadline, not the customer's. Every pricing change since gets sixty days' notice and a personal call to larger customers before any email goes out; he still keeps that customer's name on a sticky note on his monitor as a reminder.

**Hiring philosophy and other advice.** Okonkwo says Runway Kit improved once he stopped hiring to fill loneliness and started hiring to fill gaps — he almost hired someone in year one purely for company, and is glad he didn't. His first real hire, about fourteen months in, was a support lead, brought on because he was handling roughly 40 tickets a day by hand through a tool called Threadline. His broader advice to solo founders: write down the boring decision the day you make it, not the exciting one, since it's the boring calls (a payment processor, a pay schedule) that get re-litigated later if the reasoning isn't recorded.

**Rapid fire and what's next.** Favorite part of the job: a dispatcher texting at 6am that the schedule worked and they went back to sleep. Least favorite: payroll paperwork. Tool he couldn't run the company without: Threadline, plus an old whiteboard app for his own use. Recommended resource: talk to Priya Chen, or find your own version of her — someone who'll tell you the truth about your plan instead of being encouraging. What's next: a rest-day compliance module in 2026, turning the "sleep in his own bed" scheduling logic into an auditable feature.
